Salary Raise Calculator Guide

A salary raise increases your pay—but how much more will you actually take home? Taxes reduce the impact, and the way you get paid (monthly vs biweekly) affects how it feels. This guide explains how to compare your old and new salary, how percentage raises translate to real take-home pay, and when to use our calculators to evaluate a raise.

What Is a Salary Raise?

A salary raise is an increase in your compensation from your employer. It can be hourly (e.g. $24 → $26 per hour) or annual (e.g. $55,000 → $58,000 per year). Raises are often expressed as a percentage—a 5% raise on $60,000 adds $3,000. Some employers give flat amounts instead. Either way, the goal is more pay. To understand what you gain, convert to a common basis and check the after-tax impact. Use our Salary Calculator to convert hourly ↔ yearly, and Salary after tax USA for take-home estimates.

Example (hourly raise): You earn $22 per hour and get a $2 raise to $24. That’s $2 × 2,080 = $4,160 more per year gross. Your new annual salary is $24 × 2,080 = $49,920. See $25 an hour for a nearby rate, or use Hourly to Salary to run the numbers. After federal tax, the $4,160 raise might add only ~$3,200 to take-home.

How to Compare Your Old and New Salary

Put both salaries on the same basis. If one is hourly and one is annual, convert: hourly × 2,080 = annual, or annual ÷ 2,080 = hourly. Then subtract: new − old = raise amount. For percentage: (raise ÷ old) × 100. Use Salary to Hourly and How Salary Conversion Works for the formulas. Compare monthly and biweekly amounts too—divide annual by 12 or 26. Our Salary Comparison Guides show side-by-side comparisons for common salaries.

Example (annual salary raise): Old: $58,000. New: $62,000. Raise = $4,000 (about 6.9%). Monthly gross increase: $4,000 ÷ 12 ≈ $333. Biweekly gross increase: $4,000 ÷ 26 ≈ $154. Use $60k after tax and $70k after tax to see take-home at similar levels. The $4,000 gross raise might add ~$3,100 to net after federal tax.

Percentage Raise vs Real Take-Home Pay

A 5% raise on paper doesn’t mean 5% more in your bank account. Taxes take a share. Federal income tax is progressive—higher income is taxed at higher rates. So the “last dollar” of a raise is taxed more than your average rate. A $3,000 raise on a $60,000 salary might add only ~$2,300 to take-home. See Gross vs net salary for why gross and net differ. When evaluating a raise, always check the after-tax impact. Our after-tax pages—$100k after tax, $80k after tax—show estimates you can use for comparison.

Example (after-tax raise): You go from $70,000 to $75,000 (about 7% raise). Gross increase: $5,000. After federal tax, the take-home gain is roughly $3,800 per year, or ~$317 per month. Use $70k after tax and $75k after tax to see the numbers. State tax, 401(k), and health insurance will lower both further.

How Taxes Affect a Raise

Federal and state income tax, FICA (Social Security and Medicare), and any pre-tax deductions (401(k), health insurance) reduce your paycheck. When you get a raise, the new dollars are taxed at your marginal rate—often 22% or 24% for middle earners. So a $1 raise might net you only ~$0.75–0.78 after federal tax. The effective rate on the raise is typically higher than your overall effective rate. Location matters: in Texas (no state income tax), a raise goes further than in California. Monthly and Biweekly Impact of a Raise

To see how a raise affects each paycheck: divide the annual raise by 12 (monthly) or 26 (biweekly). A $3,600 raise adds $300 per month gross, or ~$138 per biweekly check. Pay frequency doesn’t change your annual total—it only changes the size and timing of each check. See Weekly vs biweekly vs monthly pay for how each schedule works. For monthly amounts at common salaries, check $50k monthly and $60k monthly.

Example (negotiation-related): Your employer offers a 4% raise: $65,000 → $67,600. You counter with 6%: $65,000 → $68,900. The $1,300 difference is ~$108 per month gross, or ~$85 per month after federal tax. Is it worth pushing? Use our Salary Negotiation Guide for tactics. FAQ

What is a salary raise?

A salary raise is an increase in your pay—hourly rate or annual salary—from your employer. It can be a percentage (e.g. 5%) or a flat amount.

How do I compare my old salary to my new salary?

Convert both to the same basis (annual or hourly), then subtract. For percentage: (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Use our Salary Calculator or Salary to Hourly tools.

Why does a 5% raise feel smaller than 5%?

Taxes take a chunk. A $3,000 raise might add only ~$2,300 to take-home after federal tax. Higher brackets mean a smaller share reaches your pocket.

How does a raise affect my monthly and biweekly pay?

Divide the annual raise by 12 for monthly impact, or by 26 for biweekly. A $3,600 raise adds ~$300/month or ~$138 biweekly gross.

Should I compare gross or net pay after a raise?

Both. Gross shows the headline increase; net (after-tax) shows what actually hits your bank account for budgeting.

When should I use SalaryWiseCalc to evaluate a raise?

Before accepting a raise, when comparing job offers with different pay, or when planning your budget after a salary increase.
